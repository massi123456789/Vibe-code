The research question

How does hyperinflation generate a self-reinforcing cycle of psychological and physiological deterioration —

and what policy interventions can interrupt this cycle to restore economic stability?

Hyperinflation is treated here not as a price statistic but as a shock to human capital — one whose consequences extend beyond finance into nutrition, mental health, and long-term hope.

The mechanism

Inflation is monetary.
Hyperinflation is a
collapse of trust.

When money grows faster than real output, aggregate demand outruns productive capacity and prices rise. Hyperinflation begins when public confidence in the currency breaks: people spend or convert money as fast as they can, and that flight itself accelerates prices.

The monetary spiral
01Excess money creation — deficits financed by printing.
02Money supply > real output — demand outruns capacity.
03Confidence erodes — the peso is spent or dumped for dollars.
04Prices accelerate — the flight from money feeds itself.

“Inflation generates stress, which may lead to high rates of anxiety and depression.” [1]

Act II · The thesis

Four mechanisms — one circuit

Hyperinflation is not a set of independent symptoms. It is a loop in which each harm feeds the next.

Act III · Methodology

A convergent mixed-methods design

Secondary evidence — the mechanism
·Peer-reviewed empirical work on inflation, food insecurity & mental health [1,11,15,17]
·Institutional reports — IMF, World Bank, WHO, INDEC
·Three case studies across structural variation: Venezuela, Zimbabwe, Argentina
Primary survey — the magnitude
·EconoChori household survey, Argentina, Apr–May 2026
·47 respondents · 21 items · 5 domains — income, consumption, financial stress, digital access, outlook
·Administered in Argentine Spanish; closing item uses the asado metaphor to reduce technical-vocabulary bias

Triangulation, not testing. The survey does not attempt statistical inference — it provides household-level corroboration of mechanisms already established in the peer-reviewed literature, inside the specific national context the paper treats as a case study.

Case study · Argentina

Stable institutions, chronic instability

Act VI · Previous attempts

Short-term relief, long-term failure

Venezuela

Price controls

Caps set below cost destroyed producer incentives — “shortages, smuggling and bankruptcies.” [12] Relief on paper; empty shelves in reality.

Nutritional deprivation at any price
Zimbabwe

Dollarization

Stopped hyperinflation, then triggered deflation. Households with no foreign savings were excluded overnight; sovereignty lost. [13]

Stalled production, new disorientation
Argentina

IMF austerity

Unemployment stayed above 9% (peak 10½% in 2019 Q2); “inflation was not brought under control.” Reforms reversed early. [14]

Household stress intensified

Each tackled a single dimension of a multidimensional problem — and each made the side effects worse.

The proposal · a complex problem needs a complex solution
Policy 01 · the monetary brake

An adaptive anti-printing rule

The central bank must cut money creation whenever inflation accelerates — with stricter caps as inflation worsens. It operates monthly and announces changes a week ahead, so households can plan.

Inflation targeting as constrained discretion — proven to lower inflation & volatility across developing economies. [18,19]
Policy 02 · the productive rebuild

Targeted labor-intensive FDI

Offer unused land, streamlined permits & favorable terms in exchange for domestic factories & local hiring — food processing, packaging, basic manufacturing. Jobs, not luxury activity.

A 10% rise in FDI → ≈ 1% employment gain across 29 Asia-Pacific nations. [20,21]

Policy 01 addresses hyperinflation's monetary acceleration; Policy 02 repairs its human and productive damage. Neither alone is enough.

Where the interventions cut in

Two levers, aimed at the circuit

Policy 01 cuts here

Price uncertainty → psychological strain

Slowing monetary expansion preserves purchasing power, easing the fear and defensive decision-making that price volatility drives.

Policy 02 cuts here

Eroded capacity → consumption substitution

Stable employment restores income and food access, rebuilds output, and gives households the dignity and mental space to plan again.

Interrupt two nodes and the loop loses its reinforcement: preserved income slows substitution, restored work lifts capacity, and the self-feeding spiral breaks.

An honest accounting
Strengths
+Addresses both cause and consequence — money creation and human damage.
+Directly ties to the paper's psychological & physiological thesis.
+Realistic versus banning currencies or open-ended subsidy programs.
Limitations
−The rule needs credible enforcement — central-bank independence, transparent reporting.
−FDI needs institutional predictability — works better in Argentina than Venezuela.
−Factories take time — benefits are medium-term, and terms must be conditional on real jobs.

The survey mirrors these limits: purposive, single-network, self-reported — triangulation, not a national poll.

In conclusion

Hyperinflation is not just a negative economic indicator — it is a cause of physiological and psychological decomposition.

The findingWhen purchasing power falls, human capital is harmed — across Venezuela, Zimbabwe and Argentina alike.
The failureSingle-lever fixes — price controls, dollarization, austerity — miss the multidimensional damage.
The pathSlow monetary expansion and rebuild productive employment, together.

A complex problem requires a complex solution — one that restores not only prices, but nutrition, dependable work, trust, and mental stability.
